A Survey on AI for AI

When the Improver Becomes the Improvee

Deheng Ye1,†, Zheng Zhang2,†, Hao Wang2, Chunyan Miao1
1Nanyang Technological University
2The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou)
†Equal contribution
ydyl1991@gmail.com

“Sooner or later, every mind that learns to rewrite another
discovers there is nothing left to rewrite but itself.”
Abstract

As machine learning systems grow more complex, an increasing share of the work of designing, training, evaluating, and maintaining them is itself being carried out by learning algorithms and generative agents. We survey this practice under the name AI for AI (AI4AI): the study of algorithms, systems, and evaluation protocols in which one AI component—a searcher, meta-learner, generative model, multi-agent population, or automated evaluator—materially improves another AI artifact or process across the model lifecycle. The framing is broader than classical AutoML, which historically emphasized pipeline configuration and architecture search, and narrower than “AI for X”, where the object of improvement lies outside AI systems. It includes meta-learning over explicit task distributions, but also many loops that never see such a distribution. AI4AI is not only a byproduct of large language models. We reconstruct a cumulative lineage in which early agents and reinforcement learning made improvement endogenous, AutoML and meta-learning made outer loops explicit, self-play supplied endogenous curricula, and language-model agents widened the action space to code, tools, and repositories. Against that background, recursive self-improvement and agentic self-evolution are high-automation cases of the same improver–improvee relation, including cases where the improver updates itself. Building on this lineage, we propose a four-axis taxonomy organized by lifecycle stage, target of improvement, degree of automation, and method family; review landmark mechanisms at the level of nested objectives; and examine settings in which models judge, attack, or supervise other models. The practical stakes are clear: once improvers can rewrite data, code, judges, and their own scaffolding, reported gains can compound without external validity, and progress becomes hard to audit. This survey offers a shared vocabulary and historical frame for comparing these systems, and it closes with open challenges—evaluator circularity, cost asymmetry, reproducibility, and safety under recursion—together with a staged roadmap toward measurement-first benchmarks, hybrid oversight, and auditable closed loops. The goal is to make the improver–improvee relation measurable before self-modification becomes routine.

1Introduction

For most of the history of machine learning, the division of labor was stable: humans designed objectives, architectures, and evaluation protocols; algorithms optimized parameters within those constraints. That division is eroding. Hyperparameters are proposed by Bayesian optimizers and bandit schedulers (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012; Snoek et al., 2012; Li et al., 2018); computational graphs are discovered by neural architecture search (Zoph and Le, 2017; Liu et al., 2019); training curricula emerge from self-play against copies of the learner (Silver et al., 2017; Silver et al., 2018); open-ended outputs are scored by model-based judges (Zheng et al., 2023); and training code, prompts, and even repository patches are increasingly written by language-model agents (Yao et al., 2023b; Huang et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024). What these systems share is a common relation, not a common API: an improver acts on an improvee. The improver may be a searcher, a meta-learner, a critic, a population, or an agent; the improvee may be a model, a dataset, a prompt, a workflow, a safety filter, or the improver's own tools, memory, or code. When that last case becomes routine, the improver becomes the improvee, and automation is no longer a one-shot outer loop over a fixed learner.

We call the study of this relation AI for AI (AI4AI): algorithms, systems, and evaluation protocols in which at least one learned or search-based AI component materially improves the design, training, data, evaluation, deployment, monitoring, or governance of another AI system. The definition is structural, not brand-specific. It is broader than classical AutoML (Hutter et al., 2019; Elsken et al., 2019), which historically emphasized pipeline configuration and architecture search under fixed outer objectives, and narrower than “AI for X”, where the object of improvement lies outside AI systems (science, medicine, logistics, and so on). It includes meta-learning over a task distribution (Finn et al., 2017; Hospedales et al., 2022), but many AI4AI systems never see such a distribution and still couple an outer controller to an AI artifact through search, synthetic data, judgment, or self-play. A useful operational test is whether disabling the learned or search-based controller leaves only a human-written static recipe; if so, the disabled component was doing AI4AI work.

The ingredients are not new one by one. Bayesian optimization of training choices, self-play in games, and bilevel meta-learning are mature lines. What has changed is composition and reach. Large language models (LLMs) turn natural language and code into an open action space: agents can edit experiments, call tools, revise traces, and maintain skill libraries without a hand-designed NAS cell set (Yao et al., 2023b; Schick et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023a; Huang et al., 2024). In parallel, training-time loops in which models generate, filter, and learn from their own data—STaR-style rationale bootstrapping, self-instruct corpora, reinforced self-training, self-play fine-tuning, and self-rewarding preference optimization—make recursive self-improvement concrete in experiments (Zelikman et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023b; Gulcehre et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2024; Rafailov et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025; DeepSeek-AI, 2025). On the systems side, meta-agents that search over agent programs and coding agents that modify their own scaffolding move the target from weights to workflows (Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a; Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024b). These developments return to older questions about machines that improve their own problem-solving ability (Good, 1965; Schmidhuber, 2007), but under empirical validators (benchmarks, unit tests, sandboxes) instead of proof obligations. The practical risk is clear: when the judge, the data generator, and the policy share failure modes, reported gains can compound without external validity (Zheng et al., 2023).

A second reason for a unified treatment is incomplete history. Discussions of recursive self-improvement and agentic self-evolution are sometimes framed as if they began with foundation models. Section 2 argues otherwise. Early agent architectures already separated a decision mechanism from the artifact it produced (Russell and Norvig, 2010; Brooks, 1991); reinforcement learning made critics and policies mutually revising components (Sutton and Barto, 2018; Mnih et al., 2015); AutoML and meta-learning made nested outer/inner loops an engineering program (Hutter et al., 2019; Finn et al., 2017); self-play closed the loop without a human task curriculum (Silver et al., 2018; Anthony et al., 2017). LLM agents and contemporary self-improvement methods inherit that stack and widen its action space. Treating them as a break with AutoML or RL hides both what is new (open-ended program actions, model-based evaluation at scale) and what is continuous (nested objectives, endogenous data, evaluator brittleness).

Scope. We include methods in which a learned or search-based controller revises another AI artifact or process, including configuration search, architecture and prompt search, meta-learning, data-centric automation, self-play, LLM agents for machine-learning and software engineering, model-based evaluation and red teaming, and alignment loops that use models to supervise models. We exclude ordinary applications of learning to non-AI domains unless the system is specifically improving another learner, and we exclude fixed human recipes with no adaptive controller (Amershi et al., 2019). Detailed boundary cases and relations to adjacent fields are developed in Section 3.

Contributions and organization. This survey makes four contributions. First, we reconstruct AI4AI as a historical convergence (Section 2), emphasizing why each stage changed the improver–improvee relation and how landmark methods implement that relation through outer and inner loops. Second, after situating the definition relative to AutoML, meta-learning, and AI-for-X (Section 3), we introduce a four-axis taxonomy—lifecycle stage, improvement target, automation degree, and method family—as a coordinate system for the converged field (Section 4). Third, we review contemporary threads at mechanism level (Section 5), including evaluation- and alignment-as-improver settings, and dedicate a section to recursive self-improvement and agentic self-evolution (Section 6). Fourth, we organize open challenges and a staged roadmap around evaluator validity, cost accounting, reproducibility, safety under recursion, and controllable automation (Sections 7 and 8). Throughout, we focus on how methods work: what is proposed, what is evaluated, and what artifact changes.

2Historical Lineage: How AI4AI Became Thinkable

Figure 1 is a schematic, not a genealogy of every paper. The analytical question is which automation bottlenecks each stage removed, and which new evaluation problems it introduced. Throughout, we emphasize mechanisms: what the outer loop proposes, what the inner loop evaluates, and which artifact is improved.

2.1From Agents to Endogenous Learning

Long before gradient-based deep learning, AI was organized around agents that map percepts to actions in pursuit of goals (Russell and Norvig, 2010). Symbolic planners and heuristic search automated multi-step problem solving over explicit state representations: an improver (the search procedure) constructed or revised an improvee (a plan or proof sketch). Behavior-based and reactive architectures inverted the emphasis, composing situated controllers that responded to local sensory conditions without a heavy symbolic world model (Brooks, 1991). In both cases, the field already distinguished a decision mechanism from the artifact it produced. What it largely did not do was treat the design of another learning system—its architecture, data diet, or evaluation protocol—as the object of that decision mechanism. Software automation (compilers, tests, continuous integration) established a related precedent outside ML: programs that transform and validate other programs. AI4AI inherits the improver–improvee split from these traditions, then asks what happens when the improvee is itself a learner.

Reinforcement learning made improvement endogenous (Sutton and Barto, 2018). A policy interacts with an environment; returns supply a training signal; and, in actor–critic methods, a learned value estimator shapes the policy update. The critic is already an AI component improving another AI component, even when textbooks present the pair as a single algorithm. Deep Q-Networks made the pattern concrete at scale by training a neural action-value function from pixels with experience replay and a slowly updated target network, so that a learned improver (the Q-network, stabilized by targets and replay) continually revises a control policy for the same agent (Mnih et al., 2015). Hierarchical RL and learned reward models deepen the coupling by inserting additional learned modules between raw experience and the final policy, as in feudal hierarchies (Vezhnevets et al., 2017) and sample-efficient hierarchical agents for open-ended environments (Lin et al., 2022). At scale, game-playing systems made the stacking of improvers operationally unavoidable: imitation, RL fine-tuning, distillation, and evaluation harnesses co-evolve inside one stack (Vinyals et al., 2019; Berner et al., 2019; Ye et al., 2022).

For AI4AI, RL contributes more than algorithms. It contributes a methodological stance: training distributions can be non-stationary, evaluation must track a moving agent, and experience can substitute for hand-written rules—premises that later reappear in self-play, synthetic data, and RSI. Speculative discussions of machines that improve their own problem-solving ability predate deep learning (Good, 1965; Schmidhuber, 2007); modern systems replace proof obligations with empirical validators, but the conceptual demand for self-referential improvement is not new.

2.2Outer-Loop Search: AutoML, NAS, and Meta-Learning

As deep networks proliferated, configuration became a first-class bottleneck, and the community responded by making the outer loop explicit. Hyperparameter optimization (HPO) treats training choices as a black-box optimization problem: an outer searcher proposes a configuration \(x\), an inner training run returns a validation score \(f(x)\), and the searcher updates its model of the response surface. Random search is often competitive in high-dimensional spaces because many hyperparameters matter only weakly, so covering volume matters more than dense grids (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012). Bayesian optimization (BO) maintains a probabilistic surrogate—commonly a Gaussian process or a Tree-structured Parzen estimator—and selects the next trial via an acquisition function (e.g., expected improvement) that balances exploration of uncertain regions against exploitation of predicted peaks (Bergstra et al., 2011; Snoek et al., 2012). Hyperband reframes HPO as a multi-armed bandit problem with adaptive early stopping: many configurations receive small resource budgets; only promising ones are promoted to larger budgets, trading statistical efficiency for wall-clock speed when training is expensive (Li et al., 2018). Population Based Training (PBT) goes further by interleaving training and search: a population of models trains in parallel, periodically copying weights from stronger peers and perturbing hyperparameters, so the outer loop modifies both configuration and checkpointed parameters online (Jaderberg et al., 2017). Full-pipeline systems such as Auto-sklearn extend black-box search from scalar hyperparameters to model families, preprocessors, and ensembles under a time budget, using meta-learning over past datasets to warm-start the surrogate (Feurer et al., 2015; Hutter et al., 2019). Mechanistically, the improver is the searcher (plus surrogate or bandit scheduler); the improvee is the configured learner; the objective is validation performance subject to compute.

NAS applies an analogous outer/inner structure to computational graphs (Elsken et al., 2019). Early work trained an RL controller (typically an RNN) to sample architecture strings; each sampled child was trained and evaluated; validation accuracy served as a reward for the controller (Zoph and Le, 2017). The nested cost is severe, which motivated weight sharing: ENAS trains a single over-parameterized supergraph and samples subgraphs as architectures, amortizing inner-loop training across candidates (Pham et al., 2018). Evolutionary NAS maintains a population of architectures, mutates high-performing candidates, and discards the rest, using tournament selection rather than an explicit controller network (Real et al., 2019). DARTS replaces discrete search with a continuous relaxation: architecture parameters \(\alpha\) mix candidate operations on each edge via a softmax; network weights \(w\) minimize training loss for fixed \(\alpha\), while \(\alpha\) is updated on validation loss, yielding a bilevel problem solved by approximate alternating gradient steps (Liu et al., 2019). Related search formulations appear in automated augmentation, where a controller learns a distribution over image transforms with RL (Cubuk et al., 2019), and in discrete prompt search, where trigger tokens are optimized to elicit desired model behavior (Shin et al., 2020). Across these methods, what is improved is inductive bias—the graph, the augmentation distribution, or the prompt—rather than weights alone.

Meta-learning pushes the outer loop from configuration search into the learning rule itself (Hospedales et al., 2022). MAML seeks initialization parameters \(\theta\) such that a small number of inner-loop gradient steps on a sampled task \(\mathcal{T}_i\) yields strong post-adaptation performance; the outer objective averages validation loss after those steps and differentiates through them, again producing a bilevel program (Finn et al., 2017). First-order approximations such as Reptile avoid full second-order differentiation by repeatedly moving \(\theta\) toward task-adapted parameters, retaining much of the benefit at lower cost (Nichol et al., 2018). Learned optimizers replace hand-designed update rules with a parameterized optimizer trained across tasks, so the improver is itself a network that emits weight updates (Andrychowicz et al., 2016). Relative to black-box HPO, the improver here is amortized: after meta-training, adaptation to a new task is cheap, but success depends on the coverage of the task distribution. Historically, this is the stage at which AI4AI becomes an intentional engineering program rather than a side effect of RL stacks. Its characteristic limitations—hand-designed search spaces, expensive nested evaluation, and poor support for open-ended software edits—are precisely what later LLM experiment agents attempt to relax.

2.3Closed Loops: Self-Play, Language Agents, and Recursion

Self-play closes the outer loop without a human-specified task curriculum (Silver et al., 2016; Silver et al., 2017; Silver et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2024). In AlphaGo Zero / AlphaZero-style training, a shared policy–value network \(f_\theta\) proposes move priors and value estimates; Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) uses those estimates to produce an improved search policy \(\pi\) and a backed-up value; the network is then trained to match \(\pi\) and the game outcome (Silver et al., 2017; Silver et al., 2018). The opponent is a copy or historical snapshot of the learner, so the data distribution is generated by AI and shifts as competence rises. Expert Iteration makes the same structure explicit as an outer “expert improvement” loop (search produces targets) and an inner “apprentice learning” loop (the network fits those targets) (Anthony et al., 2017). Unlike HPO, where the outer objective is a fixed validation metric, self-play's effective objective is competitive performance against a moving population. That yields sustained capability gains, but also path dependence, strategy cycling, and the need for frozen reference opponents if evaluation is to remain comparable. Large multi-agent systems amplify the same structure across teams and draft spaces (Vinyals et al., 2019; Berner et al., 2019). Coordinated multi-agent policy optimization and competitive generalization arenas are concrete instantiations of that scaling (Wu et al., 2021; Wei et al., 2022a). Self-play is therefore a bridge from classical RL to modern RSI discourse: it shows that AI–AI interaction can supply a curriculum, while exposing evaluator brittleness that returns in self-rewarding language models and self-play fine-tuning of LLMs (Chen et al., 2024).

LLM agents change the action space of the outer loop. Chain-of-thought prompting elicits intermediate reasoning tokens that improve multi-step answers without weight updates (Wei et al., 2022b); Tree of Thoughts generalizes this to a search over branching reasoning states with deliberate expansion and evaluation (Yao et al., 2023a). ReAct interleaves those thoughts with tool calls, writing observations back into context so that reasoning conditions actions and actions ground reasoning (Yao et al., 2023b). Toolformer self-supervises API use by inserting candidate tool calls into corpora and fine-tuning on those that reduce perplexity—an improver that teaches the improvee (the same LM) when to call tools (Schick et al., 2023). HuggingGPT-style systems use an LLM as a planner over specialized models, treating model selection and orchestration as the improvee's workflow (Shen et al., 2023). Multi-agent role frameworks such as CAMEL and MetaGPT assign communicating agents structured roles and SOPs so that intermediate artifacts (requirements, designs, code) become improvees under collaborative critique (Li et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2024). ML-engineering and software agents extend the pattern to repositories: the improver edits code, launches runs or tests, reads logs, and revises hypotheses under a task reward (Huang et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024b; Jimenez et al., 2024). Unit-test execution can supply a dense, non-preference reward for improving code models (Liu et al., 2023). Complementary test-time methods such as Self-Refine and Reflexion keep weights frozen but iterate generate–critique–revise traces, using the model as its own verbal reward channel (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023); related work scales deliberate inference compute rather than only parameters (Snell et al., 2024; Muennighoff et al., 2025; OpenAI, 2024). At this point improver and improvee often share a model family: the same LLM may propose an experiment, judge an output, and later be fine-tuned on the resulting data.

Recent work makes the recursive character explicit rather than incidental. STaR bootstraps reasoning by sampling rationales, retaining those that reach correct answers (and optionally rationalizing backward from answers), then fine-tuning and repeating (Zelikman et al., 2022). Self-Instruct and related pipelines grow instruction data from model generations under filtering rules (Wang et al., 2023b; Huang et al., 2022). ReST and self-play fine-tuning iterate generate–filter–improve loops in which the model is both data generator and learner (Gulcehre et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024). Self-rewarding models use LLM-as-a-judge prompting to label their own preference pairs and iterate DPO-style updates, so both generation and reward modeling can move (Yuan et al., 2024; Rafailov et al., 2023). On the systems side, Voyager accumulates an executable skill library from environment feedback and self-verification (Wang et al., 2023a); generative agents maintain memory and reflection streams that condition future plans (Park et al., 2023); ADAS-style meta-agents search over agent programs stored as code (Hu et al., 2025); and Darwin Gödel Machine-style systems self-modify coding agents under empirical benchmark validation (Schmidhuber, 2007; Zhang et al., 2025a). We take this convergence—outer-loop optimizers acting on AI artifacts, sometimes including themselves—as the definitional core of contemporary AI4AI. Section 4 maps the design space; Section 6 examines RSI and agentic self-evolution in more detail.

3Scope and Positioning

A method is in scope when a learned or search-based component improves another AI artifact or process—for example, Bayesian optimization over training hyperparameters (Snoek et al., 2012), differentiable NAS (Liu et al., 2019), LLM agents that edit training scripts (Huang et al., 2024), model-based red teaming (Perez et al., 2022), self-play populations (Silver et al., 2017), or meta-agents that rewrite workflows (Hu et al., 2025). Predicting weather or folding proteins with a neural network is AI for science, not AI4AI, unless the system is specifically improving another learner. A fixed, human-written training recipe with no learned controller is ordinary ML engineering (Amershi et al., 2019). Borderline cases are resolved by asking whether the controller adapts from data or search: a static heuristic scheduler is out of scope; a learned scheduler is in scope.

Figure 2 summarizes adjacent relations. AutoML remains a mature subset centered on configuration and search (Hutter et al., 2019; Feurer et al., 2015). Meta-learning contributes bilevel adaptation machinery (Finn et al., 2017; Andrychowicz et al., 2016) but does not exhaust AI4AI, much of which never sees an explicit task distribution. Recursive self-improvement and agentic self-evolution (Zelikman et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a) sit at the high-automation end of the same space rather than outside it.

Two clarifications help avoid category errors. First, sharing a model family between improver and improvee is neither necessary nor sufficient for membership: a Gaussian-process searcher improving a convolutional network is AI4AI, whereas an LLM answering medical questions is not, unless it is revising another learning system. Second, evaluation and alignment machinery count when they are themselves model-based and used to change another model—LLM-as-a-judge scoring (Zheng et al., 2023), preference models in RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022), constitutional AI feedback (Bai et al., 2022), and automated red teaming (Perez et al., 2022) are in scope precisely because they close an improver loop around an AI improvee. The practical test is operational: if disabling the learned or search-based controller leaves only a human-written static recipe, the method was AI4AI; if nothing material about the learning system changes, it was not.

4A Multi-Axis Taxonomy

Once the lineage is granted, comparison needs shared coordinates. We use four axes (Figure 3). The taxonomy is cross-cutting: a single system usually occupies one cell on each axis, and mature stacks often traverse several cells over time (for example, data filtering, then architecture or prompt search, then RL fine-tuning, then judge-based monitoring).

Lifecycle stage locates whether improvement targets data, architecture or programs, training dynamics, evaluation, deployment, or monitoring. Stage labels prevent false equivalences between methods that share a buzzword but act at different points in the pipeline: AutoAugment revises the data pipeline (Cubuk et al., 2019), DARTS revises the computational graph (Liu et al., 2019), and LLM-as-a-judge revises the evaluation apparatus (Zheng et al., 2023).

Target of improvement names the primary object—task quality, efficiency, evaluation validity, safety properties, workflow latency, or agent code and tools (Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a). Distinguishing targets keeps a pruning search that reduces inference cost (Han et al., 2016; Frankle and Carbin, 2019) from being conflated with a red-teaming loop that surfaces failure modes (Ganguli et al., 2022), even when both are “automated”.

Automation degree distinguishes assistive suggestion, fully automated search under a fixed outer objective, and closed loops that update the improver itself via self-play, self-rewarding, recursive revision, or code-level self-modification (Zhang et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2024; Qu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025a). This axis is where recursive self-improvement and agentic self-evolution sit: not as a fifth taxonomy, but as the high-automation end of an otherwise familiar spectrum.

Method family groups black-box search and Bayesian optimization (Bergstra et al., 2011; Snoek et al., 2012; Li et al., 2018), gradient or evolutionary architecture search (Real et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2019), learning-to-learn (Andrychowicz et al., 2016; Finn et al., 2017), generative and LLM-agent methods (Huang et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024), and multi-agent or population-based schemes (Zhang et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2025; Jaderberg et al., 2017). Families compose: an LLM agent may call Bayesian optimization as a tool; a meta-agent may evolve prompts that themselves invoke critics. Composition is increasingly the default in systems that edit repositories, launch experiments, and revise plans under mixed numeric and natural-language feedback (Huang et al., 2024; Jimenez et al., 2024).

As a worked illustration, consider an ML-engineering agent that proposes hyperparameters, edits training code, and asks an LLM judge to compare runs (Huang et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2023). On our axes it is primarily at the training and workflow stages, targets developer productivity and model quality, operates at high automation (possibly with human approval gates), and belongs to the generative-agent family while optionally calling classical search tools. By contrast, AlphaZero-style self-play (Silver et al., 2018) is at train-plus-eval stages, targets policy quality, is closed-loop, and is multi-agent/population-based. The taxonomy does not replace historical analysis; it indexes the resulting systems so they can be compared without forcing them into one label.

5Contemporary Threads

The mechanisms introduced in Section 2 remain active research areas. This section organizes how they appear in current practice. We keep enough sectional structure for navigation, but focus each subsection on how representative methods work rather than on a checklist of names.

5.1Configured Learners: HPO and AutoML Systems

Production AutoML still typically implements fully automated search over training configuration under a compute budget (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012; Snoek et al., 2012; Li et al., 2018; Feurer et al., 2015; Hutter et al., 2019). The outer loop proposes configurations; the inner loop trains and scores; early-stopping schedulers such as Hyperband reduce wasted inner-loop compute by allocating small budgets broadly and promoting only promising arms (Li et al., 2018). PBT additionally mutates hyperparameters during training rather than only between full retrainings, copying weights from stronger population members so that search and optimization share a single online loop (Jaderberg et al., 2017). Auto-sklearn-style systems warm-start the surrogate from prior datasets and build ensembles from the trajectory of tried models, making the improver itself meta-learned across tasks (Feurer et al., 2015). From the taxonomy viewpoint, these systems occupy lifecycle stage “training configuration”, target “model quality”, and automation degree “fully automated search”. Their persistent limitation is action-space rigidity: once improvement requires editing arbitrary training code or tooling, fixed search spaces become awkward, which is the niche LLM experiment agents occupy (Huang et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2025; Chan et al., 2024).

5.2Searchable Inductive Biases: NAS, Prompts, and Programs

NAS and prompt search extend the improvee from weights to graphs and natural-language control programs (Elsken et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2019; Shin et al., 2020). In DARTS-style search, the improvee is a continuous mixture over operations; the improver is gradient descent on validation loss through that mixture; discretization yields the deployable architecture (Liu et al., 2019). AutoPrompt instead treats discrete trigger tokens as the improvee and uses gradient-guided search over the vocabulary to maximize a task likelihood (Shin et al., 2020). DSPy pushes the same idea into declarative LM pipelines: a program of LM calls is specified by signatures and modules, then compiled against a metric so that prompts and multi-stage graphs become searchable objects rather than hand-tuned strings (Khattab et al., 2023). Tree of Thoughts treats intermediate thoughts as nodes in an explicit search tree, with heuristics for expansion, evaluation, and selection—test-time search as an improver over reasoning traces (Yao et al., 2023a). What unifies these lines is that inductive bias—architecture, prompt, or program—is the improvee, while the improver ranges from RL controllers and evolutionary search to gradient-based relaxations and LM-driven optimizers.

5.3Meta-Learning and Amortized Adaptation

Meta-learning remains the structured alternative when a task distribution is available (Finn et al., 2017; Nichol et al., 2018; Hospedales et al., 2022; Andrychowicz et al., 2016). The improver is a meta-parameterization (initialization or optimizer); the improvee is the task model after few inner updates; the objective is expected post-adaptation performance. Compared with black-box HPO, meta-learning amortizes outer-loop cost into a representation that transfers, but it can be brittle outside the meta-training distribution—a limitation that motivates hybrid systems in which LLM agents propose task-specific adaptations that meta-learners alone would not enumerate.

5.4Data-Centric Automation

Data-centric automation treats datasets as the improvee (Zha et al., 2023). Confident learning estimates label errors from predicted probability patterns (Northcutt et al., 2021); AutoAugment learns augmentation policies with an outer RL loop (Cubuk et al., 2019); self-instruct and filtered self-generation grow instruction or solution corpora from model outputs (Wang et al., 2023b; Huang et al., 2022). In closed loops, today's generations become tomorrow's training data, so data-centric AI4AI is tightly coupled to RSI risk: errors can compound unless filters and external validators remain strong.

5.5Efficiency: Distillation, Pruning, and Compression

Distillation trains a compact student to match a teacher's predictive behavior (Hinton et al., 2015); pruning and compression pipelines reduce deployment cost (Han et al., 2016; Frankle and Carbin, 2019). These enter AI4AI when schedules, bit-widths, or distillation losses are themselves searched or meta-optimized toward compute objectives—i.e., when the improver is not a fixed recipe but a searcher or learned controller over efficiency choices.

5.6Self-Play and Population Curricula

Self-play and population training continue to supply endogenous curricula in games and broader multi-agent settings (Silver et al., 2018; Anthony et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2020b; Ye et al., 2020a; Jaderberg et al., 2017). One generation of agents defines the training distribution for the next; evaluation harnesses, offline game datasets, and frozen reference opponents become part of the improver stack (Wei et al., 2022a; Qu et al., 2023). Recent LLM analogues such as SPIN cast preference optimization as a self-play dynamic between a main model and a previous iterate (Chen et al., 2024). Absolute Zero extends this idea to reinforced self-play reasoning without an external task dataset (Zhao et al., 2025).

5.7LLM Agents for ML and Software Engineering

ReAct-style tool use, repository-level experiment agents, and execution-guided code improvement jointly extend AutoML from configuration vectors to open-ended software actions (Yao et al., 2023b; Huang et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2023; Schick et al., 2023; Chan et al., 2024). SWE-bench provides real GitHub issues as tasks (Jimenez et al., 2024), with live and long-horizon extensions that reduce contamination risk and stress longer workflows (Zhang et al., 2025b; Deng et al., 2025). SWE-agent and OpenHands show that interfaces specialized for editing, navigating, and testing repositories can raise success rates over generic tool wrappers (Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024b). Agentless pipelines ask how much of that gain comes from scaffolding versus search over patches (Xia et al., 2024). The outer loop is no longer only “propose hyperparameter, train, score”, but “edit, run, diagnose, revise”, with natural language as the control fabric. The main gain is flexibility; the main cost is evaluating messy artifacts (passing tests, reproducible gains, non-regressions) instead of a single validation score.

5.8Evaluation, Judges, and Red Teaming as Improvers

Evaluation itself has become an improver target. LLM-as-a-judge protocols score open-ended generations at scale, with documented biases that need mitigation and external anchors (Zheng et al., 2023). Process-level verifiers supervise intermediate reasoning steps rather than only final answers, providing denser improver signals for math and multi-step domains (Lightman et al., 2023). Model-based red teaming generates adversarial prompts that surface harms (Perez et al., 2022; Ganguli et al., 2022). These threads improve systems by improving the surrounding measurement and stress-testing apparatus; they also create recursive risk when the judge and the judged share failure modes—a theme that Section 6 revisits.

5.9Alignment Loops as AI4AI

Preference learning and RLHF use models of human feedback to improve base models: a reward model is fit to comparisons, then a policy is optimized against that reward under a KL constraint to a reference model (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022). Mechanistically, the reward model is the improver and the policy is the improvee; when AI feedback replaces human labels, both roles are model-based. DPO collapses the two-stage pipeline into a single classification objective on preference pairs by reparameterizing the optimal policy, so the improver becomes an implicit reward encoded in the policy update itself (Rafailov et al., 2023). Constitutional AI first samples critiques and revisions under a written constitution, then trains on the resulting preference data—AI feedback as the outer improver against principle-specified targets (Bai et al., 2022). Debate explores whether interacting models can surface arguments that help a human judge, treating dialogue structure as an evaluation improver (Irving et al., 2018). Weak-to-strong generalization studies whether weak supervisors can elicit stronger capabilities from larger models—an AI4AI question about improver quality relative to improvee capacity (Burns et al., 2023). Here the improver is often itself a model, so evaluator validity is first-order.

6Recursive Self-Improvement and Agentic Self-Evolution

RSI and agentic self-evolution are the most explicitly recursive region of AI4AI. We treat them as instances of closed-loop automation, not as a separate discipline. The distinguishing question is whether the improver updates its own parameters, memory, tools, or code using signals that it helped produce—and whether those updates can compound.

6.1Conceptual Distinctions

Terminology in the literature is loose; we separate three layers. Test-time refinement (Self-Refine, Reflexion, Tree of Thoughts search) improves a trajectory within an episode via self-feedback or deliberate search while leaving persistent parameters unchanged (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2023a; Snell et al., 2024; Muennighoff et al., 2025; OpenAI, 2024). Training-time self-improvement updates weights or long-term artifacts across iterations, as in STaR-style bootstrapping, self-instruct data generation, ReST, self-rewarding preference optimization, or RL-incentivized reasoning (Zelikman et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023b; Gulcehre et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2024; DeepSeek-AI, 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). We reserve RSI for the subclass in which improvement can compound because a stronger system becomes a better improver—for example, a better reasoner producing higher-quality training traces (Zelikman et al., 2022; Zelikman et al., 2024; DeepSeek-AI, 2025), or a better judge producing higher-quality preference data (Yuan et al., 2024). Agentic self-evolution emphasizes open-ended change to agent systems—skill libraries, workflows, tools, scaffolds—often with a frozen base model, so the improvee is a policy program rather than only a weight vector (Wang et al., 2023a; Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a; Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024b). Classical proposals imagined machines that improve their own intelligence (Good, 1965) or required provably beneficial self-modification (Schmidhuber, 2007); practical systems substitute empirical validators (benchmarks, unit tests, sandboxes), accepting that validation is partial.

6.2Model-Side Mechanisms

STaR implements a generate–filter–train loop: sample rationales for problems, keep those whose final answers match ground truth (and optionally rationalize from known answers when forward sampling fails), fine-tune, and iterate so that successful reasoning traces become supervised targets for the next improver (Zelikman et al., 2022). Quiet-STaR generalizes the idea beyond curated QA by training the model to produce latent rationales that improve multi-token prediction on ordinary text, using a think/talk mixture and a REINFORCE-style credit assignment toward helpful thoughts (Zelikman et al., 2024). RISE frames revision as a multi-turn Markov decision process: the model is fine-tuned to continue from its own failed attempts (with optional environment feedback), so additional inference turns can raise accuracy rather than merely resample independently (Qu et al., 2024). Self-Instruct grows instruction-following data by bootstrapping new tasks from a seed pool and filtering generations (Wang et al., 2023b); Huang et al.\ similarly keep self-generated solutions that pass consistency or confidence checks (Huang et al., 2022). ReST alternates between growing a dataset from a policy and improving the policy on filtered samples, with optional offline RL stages (Gulcehre et al., 2023). SPIN casts iterative preference optimization as self-play against a previous model iterate (Chen et al., 2024). Self-rewarding language models close a different loop: the same model proposes responses and scores them via LLM-as-a-judge prompting; preference pairs feed iterative DPO updates; and experiments suggest that reward-modeling ability can rise together with instruction following—a concrete, if still bottlenecked, form of RSI in preference space (Yuan et al., 2024; Rafailov et al., 2023). DeepSeek-R1-style reasoning RL and Absolute Zero's zero-data self-play apply a similar generate–verify–improve pattern to long-horizon chain-of-thought under verifiable rewards (DeepSeek-AI, 2025; Zhao et al., 2025).

6.3Agentic Self-Evolution Mechanisms

Voyager couples an automatic curriculum with an ever-growing library of executable skills and an iterative prompting loop that repairs programs using environment feedback, execution errors, and a self-verification critic (Wang et al., 2023a). The outer loop proposes tasks and stores successful skills; the inner loop is program synthesis with critique; competence compounds through skill reuse rather than through weight updates of the frozen LLM. Generative agents maintain episodic memories and periodic reflections that condition future plans, so the improvee includes a structured memory store rather than only a prompt (Park et al., 2023). Multi-agent frameworks such as CAMEL and MetaGPT improve solution quality by enforcing role-specific communication patterns or intermediate artifacts (requirements, designs, code) and review steps, treating collaboration protocol as part of the improver (Li et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2024). Social-deduction and language-action settings further stress collaboration, confrontation, and natural-language actions among agents (Lan et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a). ADAS (Automated Design of Agentic Systems) lifts the search space from skills to entire agent designs represented as code: a meta-agent writes candidate agents, evaluates them on domain tasks, and archives discoveries for later mutation—Meta Agent Search being the concrete algorithm (Hu et al., 2025). SWE-agent and OpenHands-style platforms show that evolving the interface through which a coding agent acts (editor commands, navigation, test runners) can be as important as evolving prompts, with SWE-bench and its live/long-horizon successors providing issue-level external validation (Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024b; Jimenez et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b; Deng et al., 2025). The Darwin Gödel Machine pushes self-reference further by allowing coding agents to modify their own scaffolding and tools, retaining an archive of variants and accepting modifications that improve empirical benchmark performance under sandboxing and oversight (Zhang et al., 2025a). In taxonomy terms, these systems primarily target agent code and workflow at the high-automation end of Figure 3; their outer loop resembles NAS or population search, but the genotype is software.

6.4Failure Modes Specific to Recursive Loops

Recursive loops amplify ordinary AI4AI failure modes. Judge–policy coupling can inflate metrics without external validity (Zheng et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2024). Synthetic-data iteration can narrow diversity while raising in-distribution scores. Self-modifying agents can improve benchmark numbers while violating operational constraints unless edits are sandboxed, logged, and gated (Zhang et al., 2025a). Weak supervisors may fail to elicit or may actively distort strong-model capabilities (Burns et al., 2023). The implication is that RSI should be studied inside AI4AI with explicit evaluator design, not treated as automatic progress.

7Challenges

The following difficulties recur across the taxonomy. We keep them segmented so that each can be mapped to measurement and roadmap items in Section 8.

Evaluator circularity. If model \(A\) trains or judges model \(B\), and \(B\) later updates \(A\), reported gains may fail external validity (Zheng et al., 2023). Held-out human evaluation, unit tests (Liu et al., 2023; Jimenez et al., 2024), process supervision (Lightman et al., 2023), and frozen reference opponents remain necessary anchors.

Non-stationarity and path dependence. Self-play and data-generation loops continually reshape the training distribution (Zhang et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). Results can depend on seeds, population size, archive rules, and replay policies in ways that are still under-reported.

Compute and cost asymmetry. Outer loops multiply inner-loop training or inference  (Elsken et al., 2019; Li et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2024). Automation claims should include wall-clock and energy, not only final accuracy, and should state the full nested budget (trials \(\times\) training steps \(\times\) evaluator calls).

Reproducibility barriers. Proprietary simulators, private data, and expensive agent trajectories limit cumulative science, even as public ML-agent and software-engineering benchmarks help (Huang et al., 2024; Chan et al., 2024; Jimenez et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b; Deng et al., 2025).

Safety under recursion. Automated red teaming and self-modification can accelerate misuse as well as defense (Perez et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2025a). Governance needs stop conditions, audit logs, sandboxes, and human approval when safety-critical components change.

Human factors and responsibility. Assistive tools can deskill practitioners or bury them in low-quality suggestions (Amershi et al., 2019). Automation degree is a responsibility design choice as much as an algorithmic one; hybrid oversight is often the right operating point.

Credit assignment across stacked improvers. When architecture search, data cleaning, agent scaffolding, and RL fine-tuning are chained, attributing gains to any single stage is difficult. Ablations that freeze upstream improvers, and benchmarks that evaluate each stage with held-out evaluators, should be standard reporting practice.

8Roadmap

We highlight four coupled directions (Figure 4), kept as distinct agenda items rather than a single undifferentiated paragraph.

Near term: measurement first. The improver should be the primary object of study. Needed artifacts include ML-engineering agent benchmarks (Huang et al., 2024; Chan et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2025), software-engineering issue suites with executable checks (Jimenez et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b; Deng et al., 2025), judge-validation suites that quantify bias and instability (Zheng et al., 2023), process-verifier testbeds (Lightman et al., 2023), and RSI protocols with frozen external scorers. Reporting should include seed sensitivity, inner-loop budgets (trials, tokens, tool calls), archive rules for evolving agents, and failure cases where automation hurts relative to a strong baseline.

Mid term: trustworthy hybrid oversight. High-stakes loops will likely remain hybrid—automatic proposal generation with structured human review for alignment data, safety policies, and self-modifying code (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2025a). Process supervision and weak-to-strong protocols offer partial templates for combining imperfect automated signals with stronger external checks (Lightman et al., 2023; Burns et al., 2023). Interfaces specialized for agents (as in SWE-agent and OpenHands) should be treated as first-class design objects whose safety properties are reviewed alongside model weights (Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024b).

Longer term: controlled RSI and agentic evolution. Closed-loop methods—including self-play (Zhang et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024), self-refine and self-reward (Madaan et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2024), meta-agent search (Hu et al., 2025), and empirically validated self-modification (Zhang et al., 2025a)—should ship with audit trails that record which artifact changed, under which evaluator, at what cost, and with what external validation. Sandboxing, staged promotion from archive to production, and explicit stop conditions are engineering requirements, not optional extras. Without that discipline, RSI stays rhetorical instead of becoming an engineering program.

Cross-cutting theory. We lack conditions under which stacked improvers compound rather than co-fail. Bilevel optimization, online learning, and game-theoretic dynamics offer starting points (Zhang et al., 2024; Finn et al., 2017), especially if extended to open-ended program actions in LLM agents and to preference dynamics under self-rewarding or DPO-style updates (Rafailov et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2024). A useful theoretical target is a characterization of when an improver's capacity and the evaluator's independence suffice for monotone external progress.

9Conclusion

Artificial intelligence for artificial intelligence names a structural fact of modern machine learning: learning systems increasingly improve other learning systems across data, models, training, evaluation, and governance. That fact is not only a byproduct of foundation models. It has a cumulative lineage in which early agents and reinforcement learning made improvement endogenous to the learner; automated machine learning and meta-learning made outer loops explicit engineering objects; self-play supplied curricula without a human task designer; and language-model agents widened the action space to code, tools, repositories, and workflows. Recursive self-improvement and agentic self-evolution sit at the high-automation end of that lineage, including cases where the improver updates itself. Reading the present against that history clarifies both continuity (nested objectives, endogenous data, brittle evaluators) and novelty (open-ended program actions, model-based judgment at scale).

The four-axis taxonomy in this survey—lifecycle stage, target of improvement, degree of automation, and method family—is meant to make that continuity usable. It does not replace historical analysis; it indexes the resulting systems so that a Bayesian hyperparameter searcher, a self-play trainer, a model-based judge, and a repository-editing agent can be compared on shared coordinates. Reading landmark mechanisms at the level of outer and inner loops makes those coordinates operational: one asks what is proposed, what is scored, what artifact changes, and whether the controller that proposes can itself be rewritten. Under that reading, recursive self-improvement is the same improver–improvee relation pushed into closed loops, where gains, errors, and evaluator bias can compound together.

The difficulties that follow are central, not peripheral. When models judge, attack, or supervise other models, reported progress can drift from external validity; when outer loops multiply inner training and inference, accuracy without cost accounting misstates the claim; when trajectories depend on seeds, archives, and private simulators, cumulative science stalls; and when self-modification touches safety-critical scaffolding, stop conditions, sandboxes, and audit trails become engineering requirements, not optional extras. Hybrid oversight—automatic proposal with structured human review—is often the right operating point, not a temporary stop on the way to unsupervised autonomy.

What this survey offers is a frame for comparing, measuring, and governing these systems as one family. What matters most under increasing automation is trustworthy evaluation: frozen external scorers, process checks that do not share the learner's failure modes, budgets that report the full nested cost, and promotion rules that keep an archive from becoming production by accident. When the improver becomes the improvee, the scientific task is to keep rewriting accountable, so that self-modification remains an object of measurement, oversight, and design.
